Understanding GLP-1

The medication behind our weight-loss and diabetes program — what it is, how it works, how effective it is, and what to expect.

What is GLP-1?

GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) is a hormone your gut naturally releases after you eat. It signals your pancreas to release insulin, slows down how quickly food leaves your stomach, and tells your brain that you're full. GLP-1 medications are lab-made versions of this hormone — technically called GLP-1 receptor agonists — that work the same way, just with a stronger and longer-lasting effect than your body produces on its own.

Is this you?

There's no online checklist that decides whether GLP-1 is right for you — that's genuinely a conversation with a doctor, not a self-test. But this might be worth exploring if:

  • ✓ You've plateaued despite genuinely trying — diet changes, exercise, and the weight just isn't moving.
  • ✓ Your BMI falls in the overweight or obese range by Indian-adjusted (ICMR) cutoffs — check yours here.
  • ✓ You have a family history of type 2 diabetes, PCOS, or other metabolic conditions.
  • ✓ A doctor has flagged your weight or blood sugar as a health risk, not just an appearance concern.
  • ✓ You've tried multiple diets that worked briefly but didn't last.

None of this is a diagnosis — it's a starting point. The only way to know for sure is an in-person exam, which is exactly where we'd begin.

How long has it been around?

GLP-1 receptor agonists aren't new. The first one, exenatide, was approved for type 2 diabetes in 2005 — nearly two decades of real-world clinical use. Liraglutide followed for diabetes in 2010, then specifically for weight management in 2014. Semaglutide — the medication most people now associate with GLP-1 — was approved for diabetes in 2017 and for chronic weight management in 2021. So while weight-loss-specific formulations are more recent, the underlying drug class has a long, well-studied track record.

How does it support weight loss?

GLP-1 helps in a few connected ways:

  • Slows digestion — food stays in your stomach longer, so you feel full for longer after eating less.
  • Reduces appetite — it acts on the part of your brain (the hypothalamus) that controls hunger, so cravings and constant hunger ease off.
  • Improves blood sugar control — it helps your body use insulin more effectively, which is why it was originally developed for type 2 diabetes.

Together, these effects make it easier to eat less without feeling like you're constantly fighting hunger — which is why it works best alongside the nutrition coaching and portion-control guidance your doctor gives you, not as a replacement for it.

It's not just about the number on the scale

GLP-1 makes it easier to eat less, but the medication is only half the plan. Losing weight quickly without the right habits can quietly cost you muscle, not just fat — which is why your program is built around two things alongside the medication: eating enough protein to protect your muscle as you lose weight, and staying mobile — building movement and strength back into your daily routine, not just relying on the injection. The goal isn't just a smaller number on the scale. It's a body that can hold onto the result and stay strong long after treatment ends.

How effective is it?

In the STEP 1 clinical trial (New England Journal of Medicine, 2021) — one of the largest trials of semaglutide for weight management — participants lost an average of about 15% of their body weight over 68 weeks, compared to roughly 2–3% in the placebo group. Real-world results vary by person, dose, diet, and how consistently the treatment is followed, which is exactly why your dose and progress are reviewed by your doctor at every visit, not set once and left alone.

Side effects — what to expect

Like any medication, GLP-1 can cause side effects. Most are mild and settle down within the first few weeks as your body adjusts — which is also why your dose is increased gradually, not all at once.

Common, usually temporary:

  • Nausea
  • Diarrhea or constipation
  • Vomiting
  • Stomach discomfort or bloating

Myths vs. facts

Myth: GLP-1 and Ozempic are the same thing.

Fact: Ozempic is one brand name for semaglutide, which is one specific GLP-1 medication. GLP-1 is the whole drug class — several molecules and brands exist, and your doctor decides what's appropriate for you.

Myth: The weight always comes back the moment you stop.

Fact: Regain is common after stopping abruptly without keeping up the habits built during treatment — which is exactly why protein, mobility, and a planned maintenance phase are part of your program, not just the injection.

Myth: It's only for people who are very overweight.

Fact: GLP-1 is used across a range of BMI and health profiles, for both weight management and diabetes — whether it's right for you is a decision your doctor makes after an exam, not a cutoff you check yourself against.

Myth: You can just order it online without seeing a doctor.

Fact: GLP-1 medications are Schedule H prescription drugs in India — legally, they require an in-person medical evaluation and a valid prescription.

Myth: The weight loss is just water weight.

Fact: Clinical trials like STEP 1 measure real body weight and fat loss over many months, not short-term water fluctuation.

Myth: Side effects mean something's wrong.

Fact: Mild nausea or digestive changes in the first few weeks are a normal, expected part of your body adjusting — exactly why your dose starts low and increases gradually, under supervision.

Why this needs a doctor, not just a prescription

Knowing what's likely for you personally — your medical history, your labs, your current medications — is exactly the judgment call an app or a one-time video call can't safely make. It's why every OBO Health patient is examined in person, has bloodwork reviewed before starting, and has their dose adjusted by a doctor tracking their case, not a generic protocol.
